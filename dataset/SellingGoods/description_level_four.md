This exercise teaches you an iterative method. You build your solution. You re-evaluate your solution. This handles changing requirements.

First, create a class diagram. STEP 1 describes a use case. Use it. You may need attributes. Then add them. You may need event types. Then add them. You may need non-default FSMs. Then add them. You may need constraints. Then add them. Revise your model for each step. You may need to add elements. You may need to remove elements. You may need to change elements.

STEP 1
Your company is brand new. Your company sells goods. It sells them to local supermarkets. You want a simple information system. The system tracks your stock. The system tracks your customers. The system tracks their orders.

You register a new customer. The customer has an address. You enter it. The customer has a VAT number. You enter it.

You want to track your stock. You offer products. You want to register these products. Each product has a current stock. You want to register this stock. Your customers place orders. Each order has one or more order lines. Each order line records a quantity. This is the quantity ordered. The quantity is for a particular product.

Your small business has overhead. This overhead involves managing payments. This overhead involves managing deliveries. You want to minimise this overhead. Your customers must pay for their order in full. They pay first. The order is sent out for delivery next. Orders are always shipped in full. This keeps shipping simple. You want the information for your accounts. Each order has a payment time. You record it. Each order has a shipping time. You record it too.

The working day ends. You then adjust stock levels. These stock levels belong to your products. You adjust them in the system. You do this manually. Orders are placed that day. You base the adjustment on these orders.

STEP 2

Your business grows. You start to offer more exclusive products. These products are not always available. You want customer satisfaction. You start organising deliveries. You organise them line by line. Available products can then be shipped immediately. Unavailable products can then be shipped later.

You need to adapt your information system. The system must track partially shipped orders. Some order lines have been shipped. You record these order lines. Some order lines are still on the to-be-shipped list. You record these order lines too. Some orders are fully shipped. These orders will also be marked. You then know something. These orders can be removed from your watchlist.

STEP 3

Your products become more popular. You start supplying larger supermarkets. These supermarkets sometimes have several warehouses. They use these warehouses for different locations. The customer has a head office address. You want to track it. The orders have delivery addresses. You want to track them.

You want to further professionalise your business. You want to send invoices to your customers. These invoices are for their orders. Customers can now choose to pay the invoice directly. They pay directly at order placement. Customers can also pay later. They pay after the order delivery. An order should still be paid in full.

STEP 4
Your business grows. Line-by-line shipment tracking becomes too cumbersome. Some order lines are shipped together. You would prefer to track each group of these order lines.

You have recently noticed problems. These problems involve your delivery service. Customers have informed you of these problems. Some deliveries are missing. Some deliveries are damaged. You want more information about the deliveries. You then make a decision. Maybe you switch to another delivery service. Maybe you start delivering yourself. Each delivery has a status. You want to know it. Partial deliveries of one or more order lines are possible. You want to track a shipment. You track it during packing. You track it during shipping. You track it during customer receipt.
